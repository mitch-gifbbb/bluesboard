Reading the board

Orientation

Nut on the right, low E along the bottom — a mirror of a right-handed diagram. Fret 12 is the far left; past it everything repeats. Click any dot to hear it.

Two colour changes that look like bugs

  • The red root vanishes when you pick I or IV. In A, the root is the 1 of A7 and the 5 of D7 — so it goes amber. Over E7 it isn't in the chord at all, so it turns red. Red means home, but homeless right now.
  • The blue b3 turns amber over the IV. In A blues the b3 is C, which is the b7 of D7. It stops being a blue note and becomes the most important note in the bar. This is why leaning on that note over the IV always sounds right.

Number from

The oldest argument in improvising, as a toggle: playing the key vs playing the changes. It only changes the numbers and what the Spotlight buttons mean — the colours never move.

  • The key — one fixed map. 1 is always A, b3 is always C, whatever chord is sounding. Use it when the whole progression is one landscape: the classic blues where minor pentatonic covers all twelve bars. It's also how you memorise the neck — labels that stay put become geography. Weakness: it tells you nothing about the change. Over E7 it still calls that note 4. True, and useless.
  • The chord — the map re-spells every time you pick a different chord. The same fret is 4 over the I, 1 over the IV, b7 over the V. Use it when you want to know what a note does right now — guide tones, resolutions, why some notes are arrival and others are transit. Weakness: nothing stays still, so it's a poor way to learn the layout.

The sweet spot: set numbering to the key, then select a chord anyway. Stable geography, and the amber dots still move through the progression — you get the changes as shape, without the labels shifting under you.

Show

These are layers, not modes — stack them. Minor pent under Full minor shows you the safe frame sitting inside the fuller scale. Major pent and Minor pent together shows every place on the neck where b3 and 3 sit a fret apart. That pair is the blues.

Spotlight

Emphasis, not a filter — everything else fades but stays visible. Numbers are relative to whatever Number from is set to.

  • Guide tones — the 3rd and 7th of the selected chord. Two dots. These spell the change; roots and 5ths don't. If you only chase one thing, chase these.
  • Triad — 1 3 5 of the selected chord.
  • Roots — just 1. Good for finding your position fast.

A way to practise with it

Set Blues, pick your key, hit guide tones, and loop I7–IV7–V7. Play nothing but those two notes per chord until you can find them without looking. Then turn the pentatonic back on — you'll be decorating targets instead of running a shape.
